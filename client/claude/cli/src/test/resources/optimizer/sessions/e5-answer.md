# How Airplane Wings Keep a Plane in the Air

An airplane wing stays aloft by generating **lift**, an upward force that balances the plane's weight. It does this by deflecting air downward and by creating a pressure difference between the top and bottom surfaces of the wing. Two principles explain this: Newton's third law and Bernoulli's principle. Both work together.

## 1. The shape: the airfoil

A wing's cross-section, called an **airfoil**, is typically curved on top and flatter on the bottom. The leading edge (front) is rounded and the trailing edge (back) is sharp. The wing is also tilted slightly upward relative to the oncoming air, an angle called the **angle of attack**.

## 2. Deflecting air downward (Newton's third law)

As the wing moves forward through the air, its underside pushes air downward. For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction, so the air pushes the wing upward. This is the "action-reaction" view of lift. The tilted surface angles the airflow downward, and the wing receives an upward push in return.

## 3. Pressure differences (Bernoulli's principle)

Air flowing over the wing is split by the leading edge:

- **Over the top**, the air must travel a longer path because of the curved surface. It speeds up, and by Bernoulli's principle faster-moving air has **lower pressure**.
- **Under the bottom**, the air moves more slowly and stays at **higher pressure**.

The pressure difference pushes the wing upward. Multiplied across the whole wing area, this produces the lift force.

Note on the "equal transit time" myth: the common explanation that air over the top must reach the trailing edge at the same time as air below is incorrect. Air over the top actually arrives *earlier*, so the speed-up is larger than that myth implies.

## 4. Lift depends on several factors

The lift equation is commonly written as:

**L = ½ · ρ · v² · S · C_L**

- **ρ** (air density): denser air produces more lift.
- **v** (airspeed): lift grows with the square of speed, which is why planes need to be moving fast to take off.
- **S** (wing area): larger wings produce more lift.
- **C_L** (lift coefficient): depends on airfoil shape and angle of attack.

Increasing the angle of attack increases lift, up to a critical angle (roughly 15–20° for typical wings). Beyond that, the airflow separates from the top surface and lift drops sharply. This is a **stall**.

## 5. Flaps and control

Pilots change lift for takeoff, landing, and maneuvering:

- **Flaps** extend from the trailing edge, increasing camber and wing area to add lift at low speeds.
- **Ailerons** on the wingtips move in opposite directions to roll the plane.
- **Slats** at the leading edge help the air stay attached at high angles of attack.

## Summary

A wing keeps a plane in the air by shaping and tilting itself so that it pushes air downward (Newton's third law) and creates lower pressure above than below (Bernoulli's principle). The resulting net upward force, lift, is enough to counter the plane's weight as long as the plane moves fast enough and the wing is not stalled.
